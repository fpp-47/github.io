Why It Is Not Workable The proposal rests on a series of assumptions that do not hold under current (or near-term) conditions:

1. Absolute unanimity and the zero-defection rule are politically impossible. Requiring simultaneous signature by Israel, Iran, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Egypt, and the rest of a “twelve sovereign perimeters,” plus nested compliance by Hezbollah, Houthis, and similar groups, ignores the depth of existential and prestige conflicts. 2. Proxy integration and civilian conversion lack credible enforcement. Channelling former militias into infrastructure boards, municipal assemblies, and economic zones while stripping military capacity assumes the sovereign sponsor (chiefly Iran) both can and will fully demobilize them, and that local populations will accept the transition without residual parallel power structures. These groups derive legitimacy, revenue, and recruitment from armed resistance and service provision in weak states. Granting them “political respect” and seats at the table while demanding disarmament creates an immediate legitimacy-vs-capability contradiction that the PSG and VFRS cannot paper over.

3.	Technical verification (VFRS) and attribution are contested, not automatic. Satellite, maritime, and sensor telemetry can detect launches and some cyber events, but attribution in hybrid/asymmetric warfare remains politically disputed. False-flag possibilities, dual-use logistics, and narrative warfare mean the “irrefutable data → automated halt” pipeline will be challenged by the very actors it is meant to constrain. Without prior shared acceptance of the technical architecture’s neutrality, it becomes another arena for disagreement.

4.	Global enforcement of the Suez Precedent is unrealistic once U.S. footprint shrinks. Secondary sanctions and total economic isolation require buy-in from China, Russia, Europe, India, and others with material interests in Iranian energy, trade, and regional influence. Reducing the U.S. kinetic role simultaneously weakens the very leverage needed to compel third-party compliance. China in particular has shown willingness to absorb secondary-sanction costs when core interests are at stake.

5.	GCC expansion and collective security transfer face identity and capability barriers. The GCC is a narrow, security-oriented club built around shared threat perceptions of Iran. Rapid geographic and political expansion to include adversaries, while transferring operational command and granting universal MNNA status, would dilute coherence and create free-rider and spoiler problems. Over-the-horizon U.S. residual capabilities do not automatically substitute for the political and operational glue currently provided by direct U.S. involvement.

6.	Timeline, domestic politics, and external sequencing are detached from reality. A 90-day compressed sprint using pre-existing assets underestimates the time required for genuine legal splicing, internal coalition-building inside each capital, and congressional action on MNNA expansion. The domestic defence-industry pivot and replacement of legacy lobbies with logistics/tech/infrastructure capital is directionally sensible but faces entrenched congressional district interests, long procurement cycles, and path dependence. Pre-electoral windows are real, but they do not compress multi-decade security dilemmas into three months.

7.	Nuclear and residual kinetic issues are under-specified. The document notes Iran’s breakout proximity and the risk of multi-decade attrition, yet the Charter itself focuses on behavioural non-aggression without robust, intrusive verification of fissile-material or delivery systems. Mutual recognition and self-defence clauses do not resolve the qualitative military asymmetries that drive pre-emptive logic on both sides.
Bottom Line.

As an unbranded Track 1.5 stress-test or internal dialogue tool, the RPSF/MEC is useful: it forces explicit mapping of incentives, enforcement mechanisms, and domestic political economy in a way most peace plans avoid. It correctly prioritizes structural architecture over process thereafter. As an executable policy, it is not workable. Participants renounce the use or threat of force against one another and commit to resolving disputes through peaceful means.

Participants acknowledge the legitimate right of all participating states to exist in peace, security and dignity.

Each participating state retains the sovereign right to maintain such defence capabilities as it considers necessary for its legitimate self-defence, consistent with the principles of this Charter and international law.

Participants commit to protecting the rights, dignity and welfare of all peoples within their jurisdiction and to promoting the peaceful coexistence of diverse communities.

Participants commit to advancing economic cooperation, development, trade and investment for the mutual benefit of all.

Disputes shall be addressed through dialogue, mediation, arbitration and other peaceful mechanisms before escalation into confrontation.

The benefits of participation are matched by reciprocal responsibilities.
